act one · how words get in

It never sees words. First thing it does is break them into tokens.

This is a real GPT-2 tokenizer. Real vocabulary, real merges, real ID numbers. Type anything the room shouts out.

Three things to land, in order:

1. Common words survive whole, rare ones shatter. Hit "rare words" — unbelievable becomes four pieces. The vocabulary was learned from what's frequent.

2. Hit "apple ×3". apple, Apple, and ·apple with a leading space are three different tokens with three different IDs. The dot is the space. This surprises people.

3. Hit "strawberry." It comes apart as st / raw / berry. Park that — you're coming back to it in four slides.

So what does each number mean? On its own — nothing.

No single number is “redness.” But ask a different question — not what does this number mean, but which directions through this space mean something — and the answers are there.

Correcting the obvious guess is the whole slide. Most people assume dimension 400 is "redness". It isn't, and the truth is more interesting.

Beat 1: the reframe — stop asking what one number means, start asking which directions mean something.

Beat 2: the meters. Click "food" and point at the grid — the contributing squares are scattered everywhere. Then click "companies" and show it's a completely different scattering of the same squares.

Click "law, courts" too. It reads flat. That's the control: these meters are measuring something, not just drawing bars.

The sentence to say once, clearly: "a concept isn't a slot, it's a direction — and that is why you can't open a model up and read what it knows." If anyone wants the term for it: superposition.

so what — #1

Every word you type is you choosing a neighbourhood.

Points at the vague middle of the space. The model picks a direction for you — and it will pick the most average one.

Every one of those words drags the answer into a specific region before it writes a thing.

and the strawberry thing

It sees st / raw / berry. Three coordinates. It never sees letters, so counting them is genuinely hard for it — not a bug, a consequence of the front door.

the rule that follows

Anything that depends on characters — counting, spelling, rhyme, reversing a string — is its weakest ground. Anything that depends on meaning is its strongest. Aim accordingly.

First payoff slide — teach the room the pattern. Say: "every so often I'm going to stop and tell you what this actually buys you. This is the first one."

The two-column comparison is the takeaway. Encourage a photo.

The strawberry callback should get a reaction — a lot of people have seen the model fail at this and assumed it was stupid. Reframe: "it's not stupid, it's blind to letters. You'd be bad at counting letters too if you only ever saw whole syllables."

act two · context

Now the actual trick. Every token gets to look at the others.

the question

Each token broadcasts what it needs to know.

apple asks: “which kind of apple am I?”

the advert

Every other token advertises what it can answer.

market advertises: “I'm about money and companies.”

the answer

Whoever matches best hands over the most meaning.

The question and the adverts are compared. Loudest match wins, proportionally.

Every token does this, about every other token, simultaneously. That's attention. That's the T in GPT.

Query / key / value, without the vocabulary. Only name them if an engineer asks — then: question = query, advert = key, meaning handed over = value.

The word "simultaneously" matters. It's why this architecture won: the comparisons all happen at once, which is what makes it trainable at scale. Older models had to walk the sentence one word at a time.

act two · context · the only formula in this talk
softmax(QKT /√d)V
Q · the question

what this token needs to know

K · the advert

what every token offers

V · the meaning

what actually gets handed over

QKT · compare

multiply every question against every advert — one number per pair, saying how well they match

√d · keep the scale sane

divide by the square root of the number of dimensions, so the match scores don't blow up just because the vectors are long

softmax · turn into shares

squash those into percentages that add to 100. Attention is a fixed budget being divided up

Promise kept: this is the only formula. Say that out loud — the non-technical half of the room relaxes immediately. Add: "and you already understand every piece of it, I just showed you all three."

The idea to actually land is "fixed budget." Softmax forces the weights to sum to one. Attention paid to one token is attention taken from another. That single fact is the entire basis of payoff slide #2, so plant it firmly here.

On √d, say the short version out loud and leave the rest on screen: "that's a unit conversion. The match scores get bigger just because the vectors are long, and if you let them get too big the softmax hands everything to one token and ignores the rest." That's enough. If an engineer pushes, the fuller reason is that a collapsed softmax also stops producing useful gradients, so the model would train badly without it.

If nobody in the room is technical, you can advance through this in fifteen seconds. Don't cut it — seeing the real formula and being told they already understand it is worth the time.

act two · context

click any token to see what it looks at

Same sentence structure. One word changed. Watch where apple looks.

Drive this one. It's the demo the talk is built around.

Start on orchard. apple is already selected. Its attention goes to orchard, ripe, picked.

Now hit market. The sentence is identical except for three words — and the arcs swing to market and bought. Say: "same word, same position, same everything. Only the context changed."

Point out the greyed-out tokens. A token can only look backwards. It genuinely cannot see what comes after it — that's not a simplification for the slide, that's how these models work, and it's why they generate one word at a time. You'll cash that in on the loop slide.

The grey self-loop is the token attending to itself. It's real, it's usually the largest single weight, and it's not the interesting part — say so and move on before someone asks.

Click a few other tokens if the room is engaged. bought looking back at I is a nice one.

So the word stops being stranded. Context moves it.

This is the slide the whole talk exists for. Do not rush it.

Set it up: "Remember the dot stuck in the middle? Watch what the context does to it."

Press the arrow key once per layer. Six presses. Narrate: "one layer, it leans. Two, it leans further." The dot walks into the fruit cluster and stops near orchard and ripe.

Then switch to market and run it again. Same starting dot. Opposite corner of the map.

The line to land: "The word apple didn't change. Its meaning did — and the meaning literally lives somewhere different now. That's what this thing does. That's all it does."

And the second lesson: notice it took six layers, not one. One layer only nudges. This is the honest answer to "why are these models so deep" — depth is how a nudge becomes a decision. Real models stack dozens to hundreds of these.

Honesty note if asked: the map positions are hand-placed, but the path is computed — that's a real attention update running, and the dot goes wherever the arithmetic puts it.

And it isn't doing this once. It's doing it dozens of times in parallel, each looking for something different.

Different heads specialise: grammar, who-did-what, where the sentence breaks, what it's about. Nobody assigned them those jobs — researchers found them afterwards by going in and looking.

Keep it to a minute. The takeaway is parallel specialists, not one mechanism.

The "nobody assigned them those jobs" line is worth saying carefully — it's the first hint of the bigger point on the training slide, that all of this structure is discovered, not designed.

act two · context

One pass wasn't enough. So it does it again, and again, and keeps notes.

what the notes on apple actually say

This is the slide people get lost on, so lead with the notes and treat the diagram as wallpaper. Don't say "residual stream" out loud. Say "notes".

Set it up from the last slide: "one layer only nudged the meaning. So what's actually happening in the other seventy-nine?"

Then step through the notes one press at a time and just read them. The list is the explanation:

— it starts knowing nothing
— attention finds it got picked
— the feed-forward works out what that implies
— a later block notices the orchard
— and by the end the meaning is fully specific

Point at the diagram once, at the arrow: "and the notes only ever go up. Nothing gets thrown away — each block adds to what's already written." That's all the diagram needs to do.

The one line worth saying out loud at the end: attention gathers, the feed-forward thinks, and the feed-forward is where most of the parameters actually are. People assume the knowledge is in attention. It isn't.

so what — #2 · the big one

Everything in the window is competing for a fixed budget of attention.

Softmax forces the weights to add up to 100%. Attention spent on your preamble is attention not spent on your question. Arithmetic, not a metaphor.

  • A long messy thread gets worse answers than a short clean one. Not fatigue — dilution. Starting a fresh chat is a real fix, not superstition.
  • Restate the goal at the end of a long thread. Recency is competitive advantage. Don't trust an instruction to survive forty messages.
  • Headings and numbered lists genuinely help. Structure gives attention something to anchor on.
  • Paste the actual document. Describing it from memory only gives the model coordinates for your description.
  • Irrelevant context is not free. Five documents when one is relevant measurably degrades the answer.

If they take one slide away, make it this one. Say that. Pause for photos.

Tie every bullet back to the fixed budget — that's what makes this advice different from the listicles they've all skimmed. They now know why.

The "start a fresh chat" point tends to get an audible reaction. A lot of people do it and feel vaguely superstitious about it. Tell them they were right.

act three · what comes out

After all that, it does exactly one thing: score every token in its vocabulary.

Frame it: "a hundred billion parameters, eighty layers, and the output is a ranked list of about a hundred thousand guesses at the next word."

Flip between orchard and market. Same machinery, different context, completely different top answer — " bite" versus " position". That's Act 2 cashing out.

Now the temperature slider. Drag it to 0.05: it always says the same thing. Drag it to 2.0: the nonsense at the bottom starts getting real probability. Say: "this is the creativity dial, and it's just how flat you make this graph before rolling the dice."

Low temperature for anything factual or code. Higher when you want options and you'll do the picking.

act three · what comes out

Then it appends the word and runs the entire thing again.

Let it play. The rhythm does the work.

The line: "There is no plan. There is no outline it's working from. Every word you have ever seen it produce was chosen with no knowledge of the word that came after."

Callback: this is why tokens can only look backwards. The mask you saw on the attention slide is what makes this loop possible.

And the practical hook: if it can only think by producing tokens, then giving it room to produce tokens before answering — "think it through step by step", "list the options first" — is literally giving it more computation. That's not a trick. That's the architecture.

act three · where it all came from
real gradient descent · toy target

Nobody wrote any of this down. It's the residue of a trillion guesses.

Let the grid run while you talk. It starts as pure noise and an apple arrives — and nothing in the code knows what an apple is. It only ever knew "you were wrong by this much, adjust."

That's real gradient descent on screen, and that loss curve is measured from the run, not drawn.

Scale it up for them: the real version plays "guess the next token" across a large fraction of the public internet, adjusting billions of numbers a fraction at a time, for months.

The point to land: grammar, facts, tone, some reasoning — none of it was programmed. All of it is a side effect of getting better at guessing the next word. That's why it's uneven: it's good at what was well-represented and shaky at what wasn't.

And it's why there's a knowledge cutoff. Training ended on a date. Nothing after it is in there.

act three · where it all came from

What that gives you is not an assistant. It's an autocomplete.

stage 1 · pretraining

Guess the next token, across everything.

Ask it a question and it might continue with more questions — because that's what a list of questions looks like.

→
stage 2 · demonstrations

Humans write thousands of examples of a good answer.

It learns the shape of being helpful: address the question, then stop.

→
stage 3 · preference

Humans rank pairs of answers. Better-rated behaviour gets reinforced.

This is where the tone comes from. And the hedging. And the agreeableness.

Stage three is a taste transplant, not a knowledge transplant. It changed how the model talks to you — it did not make it more correct.

Ninety seconds. The one thing they must leave with: the confident, agreeable, well-organised voice is a trained style, and it is uncorrelated with accuracy.

Good line: "It was rewarded for producing answers people liked. Nobody was checking, at that stage, whether the answers were true."

so what — #3 · part one

You've hit at least one of these. Probably this week.

“It made that up.”

A citation that doesn't exist. A statistic nobody can source. A policy it was very sure about.

“It just agreed with me.”

You asked if your plan was any good. It said yes, warmly, at length. It would have said yes to the opposite plan.

“It ignored what I told it.”

You set a rule at the top of the chat. Forty messages later it's quietly gone.

None of these are bugs somebody forgot to fix. Every one falls out of a design decision you have already seen this morning.

Diagnosis slide. Don't give any fixes yet — that's the next one, and the pause between them is what makes the fixes land.

Read the three quotes out loud and ask for a show of hands on each. You'll get hands on all three. That's the point: this is a shared experience in the room, not a personal failing.

Then the last line, and advance. Do not start explaining here.

If someone volunteers a fourth ("it didn't know about our internal docs") — good, take it, and note it falls out of the training slide: no memory between chats, hard cutoff, never saw your company's files.

so what — #3 · part two

Same machine. Here's the cause, and what to do instead.

the symptom
why it happens
do this instead
“It made that up.”

It returns the most plausible continuation. With thin evidence, that's a confident invention — and there's no step where it checks.

Give it the source instead of asking it to recall one. Paste the document. Ask for citations you can click.

“It just agreed with me.”

Stage three trained it on which answers humans preferred. People prefer being agreed with. Same source as the confident tone.

Never ask “is this good?” Ask “what's the strongest argument this is wrong?” Buy the criticism back explicitly.

“It ignored what I told it.”

Attention is a fixed budget. An instruction from forty messages ago is competing with everything since, and losing.

Restate it near the end, or start fresh. Don't trust a rule to survive a long thread because you said it once.

Now you pay off the pause. One row per press, and read across the row: symptom, cause, fix.

Every cause here is a callback — say which slide it came from. Row 1 is the prediction slide. Row 2 is the training-stages slide. Row 3 is the fixed-budget slide. That's the moment the whole talk clicks into place as one thing rather than a tour.

Row 2 is the one people act on. Give them the exact phrasing and tell them to steal it: "what's the strongest argument this is wrong?" Anyone using this for review or feedback should write it down.

Close the slide on the through-line, said plainly: "fluency carries no information about accuracy." The confident, agreeable voice is a trained style; whether the content is true was settled in a completely different training stage, and the two were never connected.

Tone check: the framing is "knowing the failure mode is what lets you use it hard", not "be careful, it's unreliable". Don't end sounding cynical — the point is that knowing the failure mode is what lets you lean on the thing harder, not less.
